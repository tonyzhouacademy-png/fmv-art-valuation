Topic hub

Chinese and Asian art valuation is a translation problem before it is a pricing problem.

A cross-cultural FMV file must translate object identity, market category, condition, provenance, and comparable evidence across languages and collecting systems.

Why Asian art valuation is different

Chinese and Asian art often moves through multiple naming systems: local historical language, collector vocabulary, museum taxonomy, auction catalog language, and insurance descriptions. A small naming error can place the object in the wrong market.

High-friction categories

  • Chinese ceramics, porcelains, bronzes, jade, scholar's objects, paintings, calligraphy, and furniture.
  • Japanese prints, ceramics, lacquer, arms and armor, and Buddhist sculpture.
  • Korean ceramics, South Asian sculpture, Southeast Asian Buddhist objects, textiles, and trade objects.

Core valuation questions

  • Is the English title precise enough to select comparables?
  • Do marks, seals, inscriptions, or mounts change attribution or market category?
  • Does the condition issue matter to this market segment?
  • Is the best comparable market U.S., Hong Kong, mainland China, London, Paris, Tokyo, or another venue?
  • Does the use case require FMV, replacement value, or another basis?

Best internal path

  1. For tax-related gifts, start with art donation appraisal.
  2. For inherited collections, start with estate art appraisal.
  3. For coverage, start with insurance art appraisal.
